\title{Label-Efficient Dark-Vessel Detection in SAR:\\
Does SAR-Domain Pretraining Beat Optical, and Does It Hold Across ViT and CNN?}

\author{John Roth \qquad Kyle Wagner\\
Johns Hopkins University\\
{\tt\small \{jroth,kwagner\}@jhu.edu}
}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Detecting ``dark'' vessels---ships not broadcasting their Automatic
Identification System (AIS) signal---in spaceborne synthetic aperture radar
(SAR) is an operationally important, label-scarce problem. Pretrained backbones
are a natural remedy, but it is unclear which \emph{pretraining recipe}
transfers best to this regime, and whether the answer is the same for
Transformer and convolutional backbones. We propose a controlled
label-efficiency study on the xView3-SAR benchmark~\cite{paolo2022xview3} that
holds a single point-native detector fixed and varies only the downloaded
backbone initialization. The study spans two architecture-matched tracks---a
ViT-B/16 track ($\sim$86M parameters) and a size-matched ConvNeXt-V2-Base track
($\sim$89M)---each with four arms: random initialization, optical-domain
pretraining, SAR-domain pretraining, and supervised out-of-domain SAR
pretraining. The headline deliverable is a two-track label-efficiency curve that
(i) quantifies how much labeled data each pretraining recipe saves, (ii) tests
whether SAR-domain pretraining wins in the scarce-label regime, and (iii) tests
whether that ordering is architecture-general. Because we compare released
checkpoints rather than pretraining the foundation models ourselves,
differences are attributable to the pretraining source rather than to our own
optimization choices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Detecting ships that are not broadcasting their AIS signature---``dark
vessels''---in spaceborne SAR is a critical maritime-monitoring problem, and one
where labeled data is scarce: the xView3-SAR
benchmark~\cite{paolo2022xview3} provides Sentinel-1 imagery, but its
human-verified dark-vessel labels are few, so supervised-only training tends to
overfit.

Pretrained backbones can help, but it is unclear which \emph{pretraining recipe}
transfers best to dark-vessel detection: self-supervised pretraining on generic
optical satellite imagery (fMoW-RGB~\cite{christie2018fmow}, via
SatDINO~\cite{straka2025satdino}), self-supervised pretraining on large-scale
SAR (SAR-1M, via SARMAE~\cite{liu2026sarmae}), or supervised detection
pretraining on labeled but out-of-domain SAR
(LS-SSDD-v1.0~\cite{zhang2020lsssdd}). It is equally unclear whether the best
recipe is the same for a Transformer (ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image}) and a
convolutional (ConvNeXt-V2~\cite{woo2023convnextv2}) backbone.

We compare \emph{publicly released} checkpoints rather than pretraining our own,
for a methodological reason rather than a compute one: our question is which
pretraining dataset/recipe transfers best, so each arm must faithfully represent
its recipe \emph{as published}. Re-pretraining these models ourselves would fold
our own optimization choices into the comparison and confound the exact variable
we want to isolate. Fixing the detector, the fine-tuning schedule, and the
evaluation then lets any performance gap be attributed to the pretraining source
alone. This situates the work in the pre-training-versus-self-training
literature~\cite{zoph2020rethinking}, specialized to dark-vessel, scarce-label
SAR detection.

\paragraph{Research questions.} With the detector, fine-tuning schedule, and
input representation held fixed, we ask:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item[\textbf{Q1}] Relative to random initialization, how much labeled xView3
  data does each pretraining recipe save to reach a target dark-vessel
  detection F1?
  \item[\textbf{Q2}] In the scarce-label regime, does SAR-domain pretraining
  transfer better than optical-domain pretraining?
  \item[\textbf{Q3}] Is the answer \emph{architecture-general}---does the same
  ordering hold for both a ViT and a size-matched CNN backbone?
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Hypothesis.} Within each architecture, SAR-domain pretraining
yields the steepest label-efficiency gains and the largest dark-vessel-recall
advantage at low label budgets (ordering, per track, roughly
random $<$ optical $\lesssim$ supervised $<$ SAR, converging as labels grow),
and this ordering is architecture-general.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A controlled, metric-matched protocol that
isolates the pretraining recipe from architecture and optimizer choices; (2) the
first two-track (ViT and CNN) label-efficiency comparison of optical- versus
SAR-domain pretraining for dark-vessel detection; and (3) a point-native
detector and evaluation aligned to the xView3 metric, released with continuous
integration checks that enforce the study's comparability invariants.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Study design: two tracks, eight arms}
\label{sec:design}

The study has \textbf{two architecture-matched tracks}: a ViT track
(ViT-B/16, $\sim$86M parameters) and a CNN track (ConvNeXt-V2-Base, $\sim$89M,
deliberately size-matched). Within each track, all four arms share the same
backbone, detection head, optimizer, schedule, and seeds; only the downloaded
initialization differs. \emph{Initialization} is the only variable within a
track, and \emph{architecture} the only variable across matched roles, so
comparing arm $k$ to arm $k{+}4$ isolates the architecture family with the
pretraining role held fixed (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

\subsection{Three pretraining regimes (data types)}
\label{sec:datatypes}

The arms differ in the \emph{kind} of data their backbone was pretrained on, and
these differ in modality, labels, and domain:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Optical} (SatDINO, DINO self-distillation~\cite{caron2021emerging}, on fMoW-RGB; BigEarthNet-S2): multispectral or
  RGB reflectance imagery of the land surface. It is visually rich and
  human-interpretable but is a \emph{different sensing modality} from SAR---no
  speckle, different geometry and scattering physics.
  \item \textbf{SAR-domain, self-supervised} (SARMAE, a masked autoencoder~\cite{he2022masked}, on SAR-1M; BigEarthNet-S1): Sentinel-1
  amplitude imagery---the \emph{same modality} as the target, with the same
  speckle statistics and geometry, but pretrained \emph{self-supervised}
  (no detection labels) on a different scene distribution.
  \item \textbf{Labeled out-of-domain SAR} (LS-SSDD): Sentinel-1 imagery with
  \emph{ship-detection labels}, i.e.\ the same modality \emph{and} a related
  supervised task, but a different acquisition/scene distribution than xView3.
\end{itemize}
Contrasting these regimes is precisely what separates ``same pixels, different
task'' from ``different pixels, same task,'' and is the axis Q2 measures.

\subsection{Detector}
\label{sec:detector}

xView3 labels are \emph{points}, and its official metric matches predictions to
ground truth by \emph{distance}, not box IoU. We therefore use a point-native
detector: a heatmap head in the style of
CenterNet / \emph{Objects as Points}~\cite{zhou2019objects}, where each vessel is
a Gaussian blob on a stride-4 response map, decoded by peak-finding with
distance-based non-maximum suppression. This mirrors the metric exactly and
avoids synthesizing bounding boxes the data never had. A single backbone-to-head
adapter maps either backbone's feature map to the shared head, so the head,
loss, optimizer, and schedule are identical across both tracks.

\subsection{Input representation}
\label{sec:input}

Every arm receives the same fixed 3-channel input, $[\mathrm{VH},\mathrm{VV},
\mathrm{VH}{-}\mathrm{VV}]$ in dB, so the input is physically identical across
arms and channel handling is never a confound. VH and VV are distinct
polarimetric measurements and their difference is a physically meaningful third
channel. Each backbone's stem adapts to this input through the standard
\texttt{timm} \texttt{in\_chans} path.

\subsection{Fine-tuning and comparability guarantees}
\label{sec:finetune}

All arms are fine-tuned end-to-end through one PyTorch Lightning code path with
the same optimizer (AdamW), layer-wise learning-rate decay, augmentation, and
seeds. We train each arm \textbf{until validation performance stops
improving}---early stopping on the held-out dev split, halting after a fixed
patience once dev F1 plateaus---rather than for a fixed number of epochs, so
every arm is compared at its own best operating point rather than at an
arbitrary epoch. The decision threshold is selected on the dev split and frozen
before touching the test or final-evaluation splits.

Because the validity of the comparison rests on the arms being genuinely
comparable, we enforce it mechanically rather than by convention. Continuous
integration runs guard tests on every change: a backbone-parity test asserts
that all four arms within a track instantiate the identical parameter count
(ViT-B/16 for arms 1--4, ConvNeXt-V2-Base for arms 5--8) and that both tracks'
head adapters emit the same output geometry; a checkpoint-load test asserts each
downloaded backbone loads with its pretrained weights (not silently random); and
a scorer-immutability test freezes the evaluation code. These make ``the only
thing that changed is the initialization'' a checked invariant, not a claim.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{xView3-SAR.} We use xView3-SAR~\cite{paolo2022xview3} via the SARFish
mirror (Sentinel-1 GRD, dual-polarization VH/VV). We fine-tune on a subset of
xView3 training scenes---75--150 scenes, chipped at $800\times800$ with overlap,
yielding on the order of $10^4$ chips---and sub-sample \emph{at the scene level}
to 10/25/50/100\% of labels, with nested fractions so the curves are monotone in
data. The 75--150 scenes we fine-tune on are split 75/15/10 (train/dev/test)
with no scene in more than one split; \emph{separately and disjointly}, the
$\sim$50 human-verified validation scenes are held out as a final-evaluation set
and touched exactly once. Detection positives are vessels of
HIGH/MEDIUM confidence; a vessel is treated as ``dark'' when its source is
manual (no AIS correlate).

\paragraph{LS-SSDD-v1.0.} The supervised out-of-domain SAR arms (Arms 4 and 8)
pretrain on LS-SSDD-v1.0~\cite{zhang2020lsssdd}: native Sentinel-1 imagery cut
into $\sim$9{,}000 labeled $800\times800$ sub-images. LS-SSDD is used only as a
pretraining source and is never mixed into the xView3 splits; its boxes are
reduced to centroids so it can drive the same point-native head.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:eval}

The headline deliverable is a \textbf{two-track label-efficiency curve}
(Figure~\ref{fig:curve}): detection F1 versus 10/25/50/100\% of real labels,
eight lines (solid ViT, dashed CNN; one color per pretraining role). Within a
track, the low-label gap over the random-init floor quantifies pretraining value
and the SAR-versus-optical ordering (Q1, Q2); across tracks, matched-role gaps
test architecture-generality (Q3). We additionally report \emph{dark-vessel
recall} and \emph{near-shore F1}---the operationally hardest slices, where any
SAR advantage should concentrate---and situate the eight arms against the
external references (YOLO26~\cite{jocher2026yolo26}; a zero-shot VLM; a
contingent ImageNet-ConvNeXt).

\section{Timeline, Compute, and Feasibility}
\label{sec:timeline}

Two contributors work largely independent lanes (data/scorer; detector/eval)
with no hard dependency, since all foundation-model backbones are downloaded and
there is no pretraining handoff to block on. Compute is one 8$\times$V100 (32\,GB)
node plus a development GPU. Because we pretrain no foundation model, the total
is modest: each fine-tune is a few GPU-hours, and the full eight-arm grid (both
tracks, with seed reruns including the low-label cells) plus the two small
LS-SSDD supervised backbones comes to roughly \textbf{293--360 GPU-hours}, about
three to four nights on the node---an order of magnitude below self-pretraining a
SAR foundation model. This lower cost is a side benefit; as motivated in
\S\ref{sec:intro}, we use downloaded checkpoints for comparability of pretraining
recipes as published, not to save compute. The
downloaded-backbone arms run first, since the main technical risk is the
channel-format gap (whether each pretrained stem accepts the fixed
$[\mathrm{VH},\mathrm{VV},\mathrm{VH}{-}\mathrm{VV}]$ input); a quick
load-and-train check de-risks the shared input per architecture before the full
grid. We target the WACV~2027 Round-2 deadline (paper registration
August~21, 2026; submission August~28, 2026, AoE).
